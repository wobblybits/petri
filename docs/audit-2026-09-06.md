# Audit: the whole thing, at `2c1c6be`

Audited 2026-09-06 on `alife-energy`, in two passes. The first read the
frame end to end — how it is split across JavaScript, wasm and the GPU;
memory and allocation; dead code; bugs — and reviewed evolution, internal
state and signalling. The second closed every hazard the first had left
open, and revised the design review after a conversation with the author
that corrected the audit's model of how the pond reproduces (§7).

Everything found was fixed in the same day; §3 lists what changed. What
remains is §7's open questions and §8's plan for fifty thousand bodies. The
earlier, narrower audit of the genome is `signal-architecture-audit.md`; its
findings are closed and this does not repeat them.

---

## 1. Summary

**The frame is JS object churn, not arithmetic.** At 10,000 bodies on the
GPU path, removing per-frame allocation from the latch pass, the harvest
plan, the deposit pack and the inertia took the frame 379 → 256 ms measured
back to back, and passes that were *not* touched got faster too — that was
garbage collection landing on whichever pass was running. The plan for
fifty thousand (§8) follows from it: the solve stays in wasm, whose cap is
now 65,536 bodies, and the remaining JS per-body work is keyed on store
slot instead of resolved through maps.

**Twenty-eight things were fixed**, eight of them in the GPU path that
ships. The worst: a preset reload on a GPU machine paid the old pond's
harvest and genome results to the new pond's bodies and left the old scent
in the device buffers. The next: killing a body scanned every wire in the
pond. The most visible: bodies spawned overlapping, and the first two
seconds of every soup were the pile resolving at speeds in the thousands —
which is what every early census had been reading.

**The design review's first conclusion was wrong and is withdrawn.** It
read early-stage census numbers and concluded the pond does not reproduce.
Reproduction here is a net's growth by rewrite, immigrants are meant to be
larval, and the spacing forces gate only the opening scramble. §7 says what
that leaves open, which is mostly measurement, and sketches the mechanism
the author wants next: weights that learn within a lifetime from the net's
need signal.

**Dead code removed:** the threaded confine worker pool and its wasm build;
the wasm scent diffusion and its 16 MB scratch, which declined every grid the
pond actually uses; a 16 MB scroll buffer that copied a field of zeros once
per pond; the hop-distance table; three unread store fields; the synchronous
frame's duplicate body. The three test failures the suite had carried were
test design, not physics, and are fixed.

## 2. How the frame is split

```
                JS (sim.ts)                wasm (solver.c)            GPU (wgsl)
openFrame   ┌ components, home, pin
            │ openForceBlock ────pack──▶ bodies[12n] invI kind scale mass
            │ refreshForceScratch ─────▶ flags pwire comp sat portFree   (cached on topology)
            │ refreshBound
            │ steer ────────────────────▶ solver_steer (samples in from store)
            │ portTorques ──────────────▶ solver_port_torques
            │ declutter ────────────────▶ solver_declutter
            │ flock ────────────────────▶ solver_flock (pair cache on topology)
            │ LOD, rope paths, rope shape
solve       │ FAR: ─────────────────────▶ solver_step_far  (≤65,536 bodies)
            │      or ───────────────────────────────────────────────▶ far.wgsl
            │ NEAR: ────────────────────▶ solver_step_near (XPBD ropes, SAT)
            │      or JS twin
endFrame    │ syncForces, damp, lengths, snap-taut
            │ harvest (credit) ◀───────────────────────────────────── harvest (last frame)
            │ graph.snap (latch)
            │ collectReadyRedexes, pulseRequests (need field, flow, state), escrow, rewrites
            │ swimCost, farming, contact damage, upkeep → kill
            │ tuneChannels
            │ CPU field: deposit ───────▶ solver_deposit (box copy in/out)
            │            diffuse ×2, react, decay, grow  (JS)
            │ autoSpawn
gpuFieldStep│ plan harvest, pack deposits/probes/blocks ──────────────▶ field.wgsl:
            │                                                            fill, scatter, applyAcc,
            │                                                            diffuse, diffuse2, react,
            │                                                            decay, grow, harvest, gather
            │ pack h, inputs, CSR, chem(dirty) ──────────────────────▶ genome.wgsl: state
            │ ◀── samples[8n], harvest got, genome out[18n] ───────────   (one round trip)
            └ unpack into store by slot
```

What crosses per frame, and what is duplicated:

- **JS → wasm, every frame:** the pose pack (12 f32 a body) once, shared by
  every force pass through the force block; per-pass metadata that is
  cached on topology (steer flags, principal wire, component, saturation,
  free-port mask, flock CSR and pair list). The taste vector (4 f32) and
  cruise/turn cross every frame because the genome recomputes them.
- **JS → GPU, every frame:** deposits (8 f32 per port, three ports a body),
  probes (12 f32 a body), harvest blocks and rooms, the genome's `h` (4), its
  inputs (8), and the CSR (`n+1 + 2W` u32). The genome table crosses only
  for the slots born since the last frame.
- **GPU → JS, every frame:** 8 f32 a body of samples, one f32 per hungry
  body of harvest, 18 f32 a body of genome outputs, in one round trip.
- **Duplicated representations of the wire graph:** `flockAdj` (array of
  arrays, JS), the wake-graph CSR (JS), the wasm `adj_off/adj_nei`, and
  `WireAdjacency` — four CSRs of the same edges. §8.3.
- **Duplicated id→index maps:** `packIndex`, `farIndex`, `scratchIndex`,
  `flockIndex`, `wakeIndex`, `adjIndex` — six maps from agent id to list
  position, all describing the one list every pass now shares. §8.3.
- **Field storage:** on the GPU path the CPU `Fields.data` (16 MB) is dead
  except for the debug overlay, and the wasm scent mirror (16 MB) is dead
  except on machines without a device. §5.

The split itself is sound. Physics and per-body arithmetic are in wasm;
stencil work and the embarrassingly parallel genome are on the GPU; topology,
the economy and the rewrite calculus are in JS. What was wrong was not where
things ran but how much of the JS layer was per-body object work around
passes that take a millisecond.

### Verified in Chrome, 10k-body soup, GPU field and genome on

After the first pass: every body has a normalised emit vector, steering

range, no console errors. After Reset: the device field reads zero cells
non-zero, every pending flag is down, the seed is queued, one frame lays
the ground, and thirty frames later the pond is running with the genome
synced.

wires in 80.8 of 96 MB; emit and steering flowing on every body; a full
ground cell reads 0.997 through the scaled sense; peak speed 132 px/s at
`t = 0.6` where it had been 1,700–3,800; no console errors.

## 4. Hazards found, and where each stands

**4.1 Spawn overlap — fixed (#15).** It was the initial pile, not the
solver. Any experiment that reads the first thirty seconds of a soup was
reading it.

**4.2 The three standing test failures — test design, fixed (#28).** They
failed identically every run, which is not flakiness, and the audit first
read them as one finding about the physics tier boundary. They were not:

- `determinism > gives the same state twice` asserted that a "mixed LOD"
  scene must hash differently from the FAR one. Its mixed scene was the
  soup under a 400×300 viewport at zoom 1 — and the soup is sixty bodies
  scattered across a world disk ten thousand units wide, so the window held
  no body at all and the scene *was* the FAR scene. Widening the window did
  not help either: a soup half-viewed hashes identically to a soup with no
  view, because sixty loners that never touch integrate the same on either
  tier. The scene is now the wired 8×8 brick with the tier line through its
  middle, which is the case the comment said was worth pinning.
- `connected > oscillator net` set `rewriteDuration = 30` to hold the
  topology "frozen". A thirty-second rewrite still begins hauling its pair
  on its first frame, and the Eras wired to the pair were towed at a steady
  30 px/s against a calmness bound of 24, with zero jerk — a tow, not
  agitation. Frozen now means `rewriteDuration = 0`, as it already did in
  `oscillator-zoom`.
- `oscillator-zoom > settles the same net to the same shape` pinned an
  aux-pair gap to within 10 px across tiers and measured 40 against 59.
  The close tier settles the pair on a rope whose rest shape is the
  port-axis cubic; the far tier on a straight chord; the rope holds the
  pair further apart by construction. The check is one-sided now — the far
  tier must not *inflate* past the close one, which is the bug it was
  written against — and the design difference is recorded in the test.

The physics tier boundary is therefore not implicated by the suite. The
rope-versus-chord difference is real and by design; whether it should be
narrowed is a design question, not a defect.

**4.3 The wasm body cap — fixed (#16).** 65,536. Above it the GPU kernel
still takes over.

**4.4 Six rebuilds of one list — fixed (#24).** The six id→index maps that
resolve into it remain (§8.3).

**4.5 `Agent.sense` scaled in place — fixed (#21).**

**4.6 String contact keys — fixed (#22).**

**4.7 `componentIds` keyed on count — fixed (#23).**

**4.8 No turnover count — fixed.** `Sim.tally`.

Still open, by choice:

- `Fields.data` is 16 MB per `Sim`, and the suite constructs hundreds of
  Sims. A `fieldCells` option would cut it, but a coarser field changes the
  steering the constants were tuned against, so the tests would then be
  testing a different pond. Left as is.
- The wasm scent mirror (16 MB) is only used when there is no GPU. A second
  build without it would trim the shipping path's heap; one build was kept
  for now.

## 5. Memory

Per body, the store holds 44 fields: 38 f64 (304 B), `chem` 134 f32
(536 B), `h` 4, `sense` 4, `steer` 3, `emit`/`taste` 8 f64 (152 B), plus
the `Agent` flyweight and up to three cached `subarray` views (~400 B).
About **1.4 KB a body**; 70 MB at 50k. The `Map<number, Agent>` roster is
another ~2 MB. Fine.

The fixed cost, after this pass:

| | size | live on the GPU path? |
|---|---|---|
| `Fields.data` | 16 MB | only for the debug overlay readback |
| `Fields.tmp` | 16 MB, lazy | never allocated |
| wasm scent mirror | 16 MB | never |
| wasm everything else at 65,536 bodies | ~65 MB | yes |
| wasm heap | 96 MB total | |
| GPU field ×3 | 48 MB | yes |

Was: 32 MB of field per Sim plus a 16 MB scroll buffer on first pin, and
32 MB of wasm scent for a 32k-body cap in an 80 MB heap. The heap grew by
16 MB and the cap doubled.

## 6. Dead code

Removed: the confine worker pool and its build (five files); the wasm scent
diffusion, its scratch and its exports; `solver_confine`; `Fields.scroll`
and the copying `shiftCells`; `Graph.hopDistances` and its cache; the three
`integ*` fields; `decodeKey`, `rewriteBusy`, `slotOrder`; the string span
stamp; the duplicate frame opening.

Kept, and why:

- The object-shaped twins in `energy.ts` (`harvestSlots`, `tickUpkeep`,
  `resetRequests`, `spreadRequests`, `flowCharges`) are what
  `energy.test.ts` drives with plain literals. Five algorithms written
  twice; removing them means rewriting a 56-test file to build a store.
  Worth doing when that file is next touched.
- `Sim.step`, the synchronous twin, is what the suite and the experiment
  harness drive. It shares `openFrame` with `stepAsync` now, so the
  duplication is the solve dispatch only.
- `NativeSolver.stepFar` (the copying form) is what the GPU path's twin and
  the kernel tests use.

## 7. Evolution, internal state, signalling

### 7.1 What is there

A body's genome is 134 floats: two 4-vectors of bases that say what it
emits and what it seeks; a 4-dimensional recurrent state `h` updated once a
frame from seven inputs (four channel readings, own fullness, fraction of
ports attached, the net's demand), its own last state, and the mean of its
wired neighbours' last state; and five linear read-out heads on `h` for
emit, taste, flocking, transport and locomotion. Every weight is bounded;
`phi` keeps `h` in (−1, 1); the emit vector is normalised to a unit budget.

This is a one-layer recurrent graph neural network with mean aggregation,
run at 60 Hz over a graph whose edges are the interaction net's wires, with
inheritance at commutes (blend or assort per gene, then mutate) and at
erasures (clone and mutate). The design record in `chem-layout.ts` is
accurate, and nothing here argues with the shape.

### 7.2 The model this design assumes

The audit's first pass got this wrong and the correction is worth stating
plainly, because it changes what should be measured.

Reproduction is a net's growth. A Con–Dup commute turns two bodies into
four, so a net that keeps meeting itself expands, and most of a mature
pond's residents are rewrite-born rather than spawned. The gene pool mixes
when nets fuse: die-off reopens ports, ports latch across lineages, and the
next commute across that seam recombines two lines. Immigrants from
`autoSpawn` are larval by design — seed instincts tuned to find a
connection, not evolved behaviour — and it matters that they stay that
way, since a body that has been through a net is at a different stage of
development from one that has not. The spacing forces (`declutter`,
`flockAlign`) suppress random collisions in the opening scramble of a
fresh soup, which is a negligible regime and arguably the right behaviour.

Two consequences. The early-stage measurements the first pass leaned on —
a 45 s census of 250 bodies, the first second of a 10k soup — were the
wrong regime, and the claim that "nothing reproduces" is withdrawn along
with the proposal to spawn immigrants from the living population. And the
right instrument is a long run of the live pipeline, sampled over minutes,
watching population grow and shrink: growth and die-off are the proxies
for breeding, mixing and speciation in this design, which is why §8's
scale-up matters to the science and not only to the frame rate.

### 7.3 What the review still says

Questions, not prescriptions. Selection is meant to be emergent from the
implicit rules, so none of these adds a fitness term.

- **Most of the genome is neutral most of the time.** Taste and the
  locomotion head act only on a free principal; a wired body does not
  steer. So a body in a mature net has 56 of its 134 genes inert, and a
  loner has the other 24 inert. Every gene is mutated every birth at
  `CHEM_MUTATE ≈ 0.021`; the inert ones walk. Whether that matters depends
  on whether lifetime learning (§7.4) is what sets the working values, in
  which case inherited drift in a gene the body will re-learn is harmless.
- **The three signal channels are physically identical.** Same diffuse,
  same decay. A lineage can choose which channel to speak on, but the
  channels afford no different meanings. `diffuseRate`/`decayRate` per
  channel already exist. A fast-and-local channel beside a slow-and-far one
  would double the vocabulary with no genome change, and would give a
  signal a range for honesty to be about. Open.
- **Eras mint.** `ERA_UPKEEP_RATIO = −0.2` makes energy from a kind rule,
  which the project's own principle argues against now that logistic
  regrowth is the world's producer. Open; a balance decision.
- **Five scalar traits live outside the genome** with their own inheritance
  path. Folding them in is tidiness, not leverage.
- **Locomotion is meant to come from energy transfer within a net** — the
  transport recoil is the swimming stroke — so `swimCost` as a selection
  pressure on cruise is the wrong idea and was withdrawn. Whether the
  locomotion head should exist at all once the stroke carries a net is a
  fair question.

### 7.4 Next: weights that learn within a lifetime

The author's direction, to be planned together, sketched here so the plan
has something to start from.

A body's matrices would update each frame by a local rule modulated by the
net's need signal: neuromodulated Hebbian plasticity, with `DEMAND` — the
fixed-function, unfakeable relaxation of real shortfall — as the teacher,
and "need falling toward zero" as the reward. Three decisions make it
concrete:

- **What learns.** `Wx`, `Wh`, `Wn` and the heads, each with a plasticity
  rate as a gene. Evolution then sets how much a lineage learns and
  learning sets what it knows — the Baldwin effect, and the mechanism
  behind "agents that have been through nets have learned through
  experience".
- **Where it lives.** In `chem` itself. A commute's children then inherit
  learned weights through the existing blend-and-assort path, the worm
  keeps what it learned, and immigrants get `seedChem`. Lamarckian, which
  is a legitimate choice here and is what makes lifetime learning compound
  across rewrites.
- **What keeps it stable.** Oja-style decay or the existing per-matrix
  clamps, so a saturated modulator cannot run a weight off its bound.

On the GPU pass this is a few outer products per body per frame; `chem`
becomes read-write on the device, read back only at rewrites, and the
`readsField` cache goes, since sense weights would no longer be fixed for
life. The dirty-range upload machinery (#6) becomes bidirectional.

### 7.5 Measurement

`Sim.tally` and `census()` exist; a long run of the live pipeline does not.
§9.3 describes the lab page that should replace the soak tests as the
instrument: population, turnover, lines and genome spread over minutes,
with colour modes (by emit vector, by lineage) that belong there and not in
the main simulation.

## 8. Getting to 30k bodies (measured; see §14 for why not 50k)

At 10k the frame is 79 ms on an unthrottled machine.

The target in this section was originally fifty thousand. §14 measures the
cost curve across the range and finds a knee between 35,000 and 40,000 — the
frame jumps 53% for 14% more bodies, because the wire count explodes once the
lattice spacing falls inside the latching envelope. **Thirty thousand is the
working target**, at 105 ms today. Thirty fps there needs the frame under
33 ms, so a factor of three at three times the bodies of the original
profile — rather than the factor of twelve fifty thousand would have asked
for.

### 8.1 Keep the solve in wasm — done, measured, and halved

The broad phase now builds once a frame instead of once a substep; the solve
is 37 ms, down from 53. See §14.

### 8.1 Keep the solve in wasm — done, and measured at 50k

`MAX_BODIES` is 65,536, the heap 96 MB, the derived caps scale with it, and
`native/caps.test.ts` holds. The FAR solve has now been measured at 50k
(§12): 62 ms, a quarter of the frame, and `disc` was indeed where it went.
`disc` rebuilt `disc_nei` — which bodies a body is wired to, a function of
the wire list and nothing else — on every one of the eight substeps. The
host repacks that list once a frame, so seven of the eight were laying the
same table again. Hoisted; the solve fell about 10 ms (§12). Worker threads over a shared memory would
split `disc` and `span` across cores; the deleted pool proved the
`SharedArrayBuffer` build works, and that is the one place threading pays.

### 8.2 Move the latch pass off objects — half done

`snap` was 80 ms of the throttled 379, then 40, and is 32 at **five times
the bodies**. It is still the second phase. Taken apart at 50k it was:

| | ms |
|---|---|
| gather free ports (map iteration, two map lookups a port) | 5.6 |
| port world positions | 4.7 |
| pair walk over the port grid | 5.2 |
| distance filter | 4.6 |
| arc test on the ~31k pairs that survive the radius | ~6 |
| `buildLatchIndex` over every wire | 11.2 |
| sort, `latchCrosses`, `connect` on ~700 candidates | ~3 |

Two of those are now fixed. The arc test called `Math.cos` and `Math.sin`
on a body's heading twice per surviving pair, and `Math.hypot` twice, for a
heading the gather loop had already turned — 60,000 sine-cosine pairs and
as many hypotenuses a frame. Each port now carries its outward axis in two
arrays parallel to its position, written from one turn of the heading per
body. `buildLatchIndex` resolved `wire.a.id -> body` with two map lookups
per wire, 50,000 a frame, rebuilding a resolution `wireListResolved`
already kept on the same two versions; it now reads that, and the frame
does one endpoint resolution instead of two.

What remains is the shape of the pass rather than its arithmetic: emit
candidate pairs from the solver's own spatial hash (it already bins the
same bodies for `solver_steer`), and rebuild the latch index only when a
wire is born or dies rather than every frame.

### 8.3 One adjacency, slot-keyed everything

The roster list is one now (#24), the six id→index maps are gone, and the
four CSRs are down to one: `refreshWakeGraph` builds it and the two LOD
tiers, flocking and the need field all read that same pair of arrays —
flocking by handing it straight to wasm, the need field by aliasing it.
Still duplicated: every wire endpoint is `{id, slot}` resolved with
`agents.get`, now in one place instead of two but still 50,000 map lookups
a frame. Replace with: `Wire` gaining cached store slots
for its two ends, refreshed on roster change; one CSR over slots in the
store, rebuilt on `(graph.version, rosterVersion)`, consumed by flocking,
the wake graph, the need field, the genome and the wasm pack. Every
`this.agents.get(w.a.id)` in a hot loop becomes an array read. This is the
refactor with the widest payoff and the least risk, because every consumer
already goes through one of the caches it replaces.

### 8.4 Stop packing what does not change — half done

`packPose` writes mass, inertia, kind and scale every frame. Only pose
changes. Split the pack into a topology part (on version bump) and a pose
part. Same for `packNearMeta`.

What is done is the cheaper half: the pack reads the store's arrays instead
of going through the flyweight's accessors, and takes the kind as the
integer it already is rather than as a string it converts twice (§12).
26.4 ms to 22.5. The split itself is still open, and it is the larger win,
because inertia is a divide and a couple of multiplies per body for a value
that changes only when a body grows.

### 8.5 Field: the cost is fixed and already paid

The field is 3 ms of GPU time at any body count; deposits and probes are
linear in bodies and allocation-free. Nothing to do.

## 9. Testing

### 9.0 It was hanging, and that was a real bug

The default suite took upward of thirty minutes and printed nothing. One
test was sitting on an infinite loop caused by a roster defect in the code
it was testing, and because the loop is synchronous no timeout could fire.
Fixed; the suite is now **35 seconds, 730 tests**. See §13 — the rest of
this section was written before that was known, and its judgements about the
suite's *content* still stand.

### 9.1 What the suite is, and is not

762 tests in 60 files. Most exercise the opening state of a soup —
disconnected bodies, first latches, single rewrites — on the synchronous
CPU path. That makes the suite a **change detector for mechanical
rewrites**: it caught nothing about the pond's behaviour in this audit and
was not expected to, but it did confirm that the latch pass, the harvest
binning and the wasm rebuild still produce the same numbers. It is not an
instrument for evolution, and its long runs are not an instrument for
performance, since they drive `step` on the CPU field and never touch
rendering, the async pipeline or the GPU.

### 9.2 What changed

**The suite's wall time was the CPU field.** Two diffusions, a decay and a
growth over a million cells cost about twenty milliseconds a frame in Node
before a single body is touched, so a test that steps a small net for ten
seconds paid twelve of those on the field, and seven hundred such tests
were most of a suite that took four minutes on a cold machine and half an
hour on a warm one. Three tests that built their own full-size field and
compared four million floats through the matcher took twenty seconds each.

`Sim` takes a field size now (`Sim.defaultFieldCells`, third constructor
argument), with the cell held at ten units so the sensor spacing, the dead
zone and the deposit normalisation mean what they meant: a smaller dish,
not a coarser one. The `suite` and `soak` projects run on 256 cells a side
through a second setup file, a sixteenth of the field work. The soak
project went from many minutes to 59 s for all four files; `determinism`
alone had been 141 s. Bench and experiments keep the full dish, since a
ledger over a small field would not be a ledger of the thing that ships.

Four projects by cost: `suite` (`npm test`), `soak` (`npm run test:soak`),
`bench`, `experiments`. The wasm singleton's caches are reset before every
test, which is where the order-dependent failures lived. The three standing
failures were test design (§4.2) and are fixed.

### 9.2b A correction this audit owes

§8 of the first pass said the genome shader "already uses eight storage
buffers against a per-stage guarantee of eight". It uses **seven**; the
eighth binding is a uniform, which counts against a different limit. The
pass that is genuinely at the limit is the field's, which had to merge two
bindings to fit its harvest buffer, and this audit carried that constraint
across. The consequence was not academic: it was the stated reason for
leaving lifetime learning off the GPU, and there was room for it all along.

### 9.3 The instrument that is missing: a lab page

Behaviour has to be measured on the live pipeline, over minutes, at the
sizes that matter. That is a page, not a test: `lab.html` running
`stepAsync` with the GPU field and genome and rendering on, for as long as
you like, sampling `census()`, `tally`, per-net genome statistics and the
energy totals into a timeline, with colour modes by emit vector and by
lineage, and the harness API exposed on `window`. It replaces the soak
tests as the place to ask "does a pond stay a pond" and "is anything
changing", and it is where §7.4's learning would be watched.

The CPU harness (`src/experiments/`) exists and works — seeded trials, a
parameter grid crossed with seeds, JSON out — but the CPU field costs
~20 ms a frame at any body count, so a ten-minute pond is minutes of wall
time per trial. It is the right tool for a quick sweep and the wrong one
for a long run.

## 10. What the literature says, and what applies

- **Flow-Lenia** (Plantec et al., *Artificial Life* 2025) got sustained
  evolutionary dynamics out of a continuous CA by adding mass conservation
  and parameters embedded in the substrate. Petri has both. What Flow-Lenia
  measures its success with is the **evolutionary activity statistics**
  (Bedau et al.): each genotype's persistence weighted by its population,
  separating "new adaptations keep appearing" from "diversity is bounded".
  The lab page's timeline is the data for that.
- **Minimal criterion coevolution** (Brant & Stanley, GECCO 2017): open-ended
  search needs no fitness function, only a minimal criterion — survive long
  enough to reproduce — and diverges on its own once many routes satisfy
  it. That is exactly the author's stance that selection should be
  emergent.
- **Honest signalling** (Számadó et al., BMC Biology 2022): honesty is
  maintained by trade-offs rather than costs, which is the argument the
  emit budget already makes. The artificial-world study of inter-agent
  signalling (*Artificial Life* 7(1)) found signalling evolves when a signal
  is useful to kin and costly through the attention it draws; channels with
  different reach are what would give a signal a range for that to be
  about.
- **Neuromodulated plasticity** (Soltoggio's line; Miconi's differentiable
  plasticity): a Hebbian update gated by a reward signal, with the
  plasticity coefficients themselves evolved, is the standard form of
  §7.4, and the Baldwin-effect literature is the account of why evolution
  and learning together find things neither finds alone.
- **Graph neural cellular automata** (Grattarola et al. NeurIPS 2021; the
  2025 developmental graph CA work): Petri's state update is a graph NCA
  whose edges are rewritten by the calculus. That literature learns the
  rule by gradient against a target; Petri would learn it locally from a
  need signal, which is the difference between a trained system and a
  living one.
- **Interaction combinators as artificial chemistry** (Buliga, 2020): graph
  rewrite systems that allow conflicting rewrites did better at replication
  and metabolism than deterministic ones. Petri's non-determinism lives in
  the physical meeting and the escrow, which is a reasonable place for it.

Sources: Flow-Lenia [MIT Press](https://direct.mit.edu/artl/article/31/2/228/130572/Flow-Lenia-Emergent-Evolutionary-Dynamics-in-Mass),
[arXiv 2506.08569](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08569);
MCC [GECCO 2017](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3071178.3071186),
[benchmark 2019](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3321707.3321756);
honest signalling [BMC Biology 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12915-022-01496-9),
[Artificial Life 7(1)](https://direct.mit.edu/artl/article/7/1/3/2365/Conditions-Enabling-the-Evolution-of-Inter-Agent);
graph NCA [NeurIPS 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/file/af87f7cdcda223c41c3f3ef05a3aaeea-Paper.pdf),
[developmental graph CA 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.08091);
mutation [self-adaptive mutation, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11249216/),
[drift-barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift-barrier_hypothesis);
interaction combinators [arXiv 2005.06060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06060).

### The finding that reorders §8

**The GPU is not the bottleneck at any size, and never was.** The round-trip
wait is flat at 4–6 ms from 2,000 bodies to 50,000 — a twenty-five-fold
range. The field costs what it costs whatever swims in it, the genome pass
with learning on is under a millisecond, and the device spends most of the
frame idle waiting for JavaScript to hand it the next buffer.

Of the 260 ms:

| | ms | share |
|---|---|---|
| JavaScript | ~165 | 63% |
| wasm (`solve`, and the solver half of `steer`/`flock`) | ~62 | 24% |
| JS packing and unpacking GPU buffers | ~32 | 12% |
| the GPU itself | ~6 | 2% |

And **no single phase dominates**. The largest is the wasm solve at 24%;
the largest JavaScript phase is 12%. Take the latch pass to zero and the
frame is 228 ms instead of 260. There is no fix here, only a long tail, and
the tail is the point: every one of those phases is a per-body or per-wire
JavaScript loop, and they cost what they cost for the same reason.

So the honest reading of §8's target at the time — 50k at 30 fps, a frame
under 33 ms — is that shaving phases will not get there from 260. (§14 later
retargets to 30,000, where the frame is 105 ms rather than 235.) What gets there is
moving whole phases off JavaScript, and the ranked candidates by size are
the pose pack (26 ms), the deposit pack (12 ms), rope shape and rest length
(19 ms together), and the two pulse passes (17 ms together). The device has
the headroom: it is idle 98% of the frame.

### What this pass changed, and what each was worth

Measured as an A/B on the same page, three runs each, first discarded.

| | control | after | delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| whole frame | 285.5 | 259.4 | −26.1 |
| solve | 67.8 | 57.8 | −10.0 |
| snap | 44.2 | 38.9 | −5.3 |
| flock | 12.2 | 8.5 | −3.7 |
| refreshLengths | 11.5 | 9.6 | −1.9 |

1. **`disc_nei` hoisted out of the substep loop** (§8.1). Eight rebuilds of
   one frame's topology became one. Worth the 10 ms on `solve`. The riskier
   half — reusing `collect_pairs`' spatial hash across substeps, the way
   `solver_step_near` already does — is deliberately not done: bodies move
   between substeps and the near tier reuses its pair list under an
   explicit staleness guard that the far tier has no equivalent of.
2. **Flocking reads the wake graph's CSR** instead of building an
   array-of-arrays from the same wires in the same order and then
   flattening it into the same shape to hand to wasm.
3. **The need field aliases that CSR** rather than laying its own. Both
   guards key on `(graph.version, rosterVersion)`, and nothing outside
   `wireAdjacency` reads the object, so the alias can never outlive the
   arrays it points at.
4. **The latch pass stopped recomputing trigonometry** (§8.2), and stopped
   resolving wire endpoints the frame had already resolved. Worth about
   4 ms of `snap` on its own; the endpoint change is roughly neutral on the
   clock and removes a second implementation.
5. **The packs read the store, not the flyweight** (§8.4, partly). `packPose`
   was fourteen accessor calls a body — twenty-eight property loads to reach
   arrays the sim holds directly. Hoisted, it is 26.4 ms to 22.5. The kind
   was the worst of them: the store already holds it as the same small
   integer the solver wants, and the pack turned it into `'era'` and back.
   The deposit pack got the same treatment and did **not** move (11.8 to
   12.9, inside the noise on a machine that had an editor at 200% CPU) —
   it writes four fields a body, not fourteen, so the loop was already
   dominated by the writes.

Together these are about 34 ms of 285, or 12%. That is worth having and it
is not the answer; see the ranking above.

Two pieces of dead code went with them: `WireAdjacency.buildIndexed`, which
nothing called once the need field started aliasing, and `inSnapArcAt`,
whose arithmetic is now inline in the pass with `portFrameInto` supplying
the axis. `port-geometry.test.ts` pins the replacement against both
functions it replaced, including the sign convention — a flipped sign there
would let ports latch to whatever is behind them, which reads as the pond
being unusually social rather than as a bug.

Everything above is bit-identical: the determinism hashes hold, which is
what they are for. That constrained the arc-test rewrite in particular. The
sqrt-free form — comparing squared dot products instead of dividing by the
distance — is faster still and was not taken, because it disagrees with the
old form in the last bit at the boundary, and a single flipped latch
rewrites the rest of the run.

## 13. Why the default suite took half an hour

It was one test, and it was a real bug rather than a slow test.

`npm test` was taking upward of thirty minutes and printing nothing. Timing
each of the 56 files separately gave the answer immediately: 55 of them
finish in one to eight seconds and total 108, and `src/lambda.test.ts` never
finishes at all. Inside it, one test — "seals the interface so the term
cannot latch onto a passer-by" — hangs on its **first** `sim.step`, with
seven bodies in the pond.

The chain, from the outside in:

1. `createAgent(id, kind, x, y, heading, params, store = new AgentStore(1))`
   builds a private one-slot store when the caller does not pass one.
2. `injectTerm` did not pass one. So every agent of an injected lambda term
   came back sitting in **slot 0** of a store nothing else could see, while
   being registered in `sim.agents` as a live body.
3. A body's slot is how every per-frame pass finds its data. Seven agents at
   slot 0 means seven bodies aliasing whatever lives in the sim's slot 0.
4. `HarvestPlan.build` bins bodies into cells as intrusive lists:
   `next[s] = head[c]; head[c] = s`. Push one slot onto one cell twice and
   `next[s] === s`. The walk that unwinds the chain never reaches -1.

That last step is why it cost half an hour rather than showing up as a
failure. It is a synchronous loop inside `step`, so the event loop never
turns, so **vitest's 150-second timeout can never fire**. The run does not
fail, it stops — with no output, because the reporter has nothing to flush.

Three changes:

- `injectTerm` takes the sim's own store and passes it (`src/lambda.ts`).
  `lambda.test.ts` goes from never finishing to 4 seconds, 14 passing.
- A new test pins the invariant directly: every agent of an injected term is
  in the sim's store, one slot each.
- The chain walk in `HarvestPlan.build` is bounded by the number of entries
  actually chained and throws when it overruns. It can only overrun when two
  live bodies claim one slot, and a message naming that beats a spin.

**The default suite is now 35 seconds: 56 files, 730 tests, all passing.**
Soak is 26 seconds, 41 passing. That is the answer to "the suite has been
running for thirty minutes" — not that the tests were too slow, but that one
of them was sitting on a defect in the thing it was testing.

Two things worth keeping from the diagnosis. Vitest's reporters — `default`,
`basic`, `tap-flat` — all buffered under `CI=1`, so none of them showed which
file was running; **timing files one at a time is what found it**. And a test
that loops synchronously is invisible to every timeout the runner has, which
is an argument for the long-running tests to yield.

## 14. Fourth pass: moving work off the frame, not out of the loops

§12 said the target needs whole phases moved off JavaScript, and that the
long tail cannot be shaved into shape. This pass tested that, and the result
is more specific than either half of it.

**What paid was removing repeated work. What did not pay was tuning loops.**

### The far tier's broad phase — the biggest single win of the session

`solver_step_far` runs eight substeps and each one called `collect_pairs`: a
bounding box over every body, a counting sort into cells, then every pair
sharing a cell or a neighbouring one written out, with no distance test. At
fifty thousand bodies that is hundreds of thousands of pairs emitted eight
times a frame from positions that moved a quarter of a pixel between one
substep and the next.

`solver_step_near` has always built its list once a frame and reused it for
every substep, on the same cell size and the same `+ 4.f` margin. The far
tier now does too.

| | control | after |
|---|---|---|
| solve | 52.6 | 37.4 ms |
| whole frame | 230.9 | 212.9 ms |

Soundness is the margin, and it is the same argument the near tier has always
rested on: the cell is two body radii plus slop plus four, so a pair left out
is more than a body apart, and peak speed in a settled pond is about two
pixels a frame. The list is a superset, never a filter — every pair in it is
still distance-tested, every substep.

### Two packs read the store instead of the flyweight

The steer row and the GPU probe row both walk every body once a frame for a
consumer that is not JavaScript, and both went through `Agent`'s accessors.
`tasteOf(agent, channel)` was the worst: four calls a body, four re-reads of
`a.slot`, and a comparison per channel against a compile-time constant.

Timed back to back on the same list, because whole-frame numbers drifted 20%
between arms on a machine with an editor and a Spotlight index running:

| loop | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| steer pack | 6.88 | 1.98 ms |
| probe pack | 6.58 | 4.35 ms |

**3.04 ms of the probe pack's remaining 4.35 is its four `Math.sin`/`Math.cos`
a body.** Those could come from the body's memoised cosine and sine through
the angle-sum identity. Deliberately not taken: it agrees only to within a bit
or two, and these are the positions that decide what every body smells, so it
would move the pond and cost the determinism hashes their meaning. It is a
real option, and it is the author's call, not a free win.

### The need map

`pulseRequests` collected three claims into a `Map` from agent id, cleared and
refilled every frame and drained with a roster lookup per entry. `seedRequest`
is already a maximum into a field that had just been zeroed, so the map was
never needed. 7.88 -> 7.00 ms. **Worth recording that the map was not what the
pass was spending on** — the remaining seven milliseconds are `rescueNeed`
walking the flyweight once a body.

### One change measured neutral and was reverted

Stamping each wire with its two resolved bodies, so that `refreshLengths`,
`syncRopeShape`, `curveLength`, `stemSpan` and the latch index could stop
asking the roster map — about a hundred and fifty thousand lookups a frame.

| | control | after |
|---|---|---|
| whole frame | 233.6 | 232.9 |
| refreshLengths | 8.25 | 7.15 |
| syncRopeShape | 6.05 | 5.55 |
| rewrites | 7.40 | 8.75 |
| snap | 32.3 | 34.2 |

It saved 1.6 ms of lookups and gave 3.2 ms back, because stamping all
twenty-four thousand wires has to run again after every latch bumps the graph
version. It also added an ordering rule six call sites would have had to
remember, which is the same shape of footgun as §13's. Reverted. **The map
lookups are simply not where the time is**, and that is worth knowing before
anyone tries it again.

### The angle-sum identity, and a hole it exposed in the suite

The probe pack's four `Math.sin`/`Math.cos` a body now come from the body's
heading by the angle-sum identity, and the heading's own cosine and sine come
from the store's memo, which the latch pass fills a few phases earlier for the
same bodies. The author asked for this knowing it is not bit-identical.

How far from identical: measured over four sensor angles and two million
headings, the worst disagreement is **5.1e-16 in the unit vector** — a couple
of ulps — which is **1.3e-14 px of probe position** against a field cell about
forty px across. The sampled cell is the same one.

| | control | after |
|---|---|---|
| gpu:packProbe | 3.63 | 1.94 ms |

The memo costs the latch pass about 0.3 ms, because headings move every frame
so its own lookups mostly miss; timed on fifty thousand bodies, raw cosine and
sine is 1.59 ms, through the memo on a hit 0.66, and forced to miss every body
1.92. The pass that fills it pays; the pass after it reads for free.

**The determinism hashes did not move — and that is a finding, not a
reassurance.** All ten are identical before and after. The reason is that the
scenarios run in Node with the field on the CPU, so **the suite never executes
the probe pack at all**. The change was verified in the browser instead: no
non-finite values, all fifty thousand bodies carrying a non-zero trail and
drive, peak speed and population matching the unchanged build frame for frame.

That gap is worth naming for whoever works here next. `gpu:packProbe`,
`gpu:packDeposit`, `gpu:plan` and the genome pass are host code that only runs
when WebGPU is present, and no test in any project reaches them. The GPU
kernels themselves have mirror tests; the JavaScript that feeds them does not.

### Pick 30,000, not 50,000: the cost curve has a knee

Benchmarked at the same age — fifteen warm-up frames, fifteen measured — so
the density and the wire count are comparable across rows.

| bodies | wires | frame | fps |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25,000 | 3,363 | 81 ms | 12.3 |
| 30,000 | 6,066 | 105 ms | 9.5 |
| 35,000 | 9,551 | 132 ms | 7.6 |
| 40,000 | 13,978 | 202 ms | 5.0 |
| 50,000 | 24,134 | 235 ms | 4.3 |

Cost is badly super-linear, and the reason is in the wires column rather than
the bodies one: doubling from 25k to 50k multiplies the wire count by seven.
The lattice puts bodies 57 px apart at 25,000 and 40 px apart at 50,000, and
40 px is inside the latching envelope — so at the top of the range very nearly
the whole dish latches at once.

**Between 35,000 and 40,000 the frame jumps 53% for 14% more bodies.** That is
the knee. Everything at or below 35,000 is on the gentle part of the curve.

So the working target is **30,000**, at 105 ms — 2.2x faster than 50,000, with
room to fall to 60 fps-ish territory as the remaining phases come off
JavaScript. 50,000 still runs, with no non-finite values and no instability;
it is simply the expensive end of a curve that bends.

Every measurement earlier in this document was taken at 50,000. The relative
A/B results stand — both arms were always at the same size — but read the
absolute frame costs as the top of the range, not the target.

### What the population does, and what it is not

Worth recording because it is easy to misread, and I misread it first.

Seeded at 30,000 the population falls, and I took that for starvation. It is
not. Over 180 frames at 30,000, with the first 60 discarded:

| | 10,000 | 30,000 |
|---|---|---|
| died (starved) | **0** | **0** |
| in debt | 0.1% | 0.6% |
| mean spare energy | +1.69 | +1.46 |
| commutes | 456 | 1,593 |
| erases | 760 | 4,294 |
| annihilations | 379 | 2,474 |
| commute share | 0.40 | 0.235 |
| population | +154 | −1,762 |

**Nothing starves at any density.** `died` is zero and the pond is well fed at
both. The population change is entirely rewrite arithmetic: a commute makes
four bodies from two, an annihilation takes two away, an erase takes what it
meets.

What density does is change the *mix*. Random pairing of a third-each kind mix
gives a commute share of 2/9 = 0.22, and at 30,000 the measured share is 0.235
— chance. At 10,000 it is 0.40, comfortably better than chance, because bodies
have room to steer, face and choose before they latch. **Crowding does not
starve the pond; it makes latching indiscriminate, and indiscriminate latching
is net destructive.**

That is a claim about the thing this simulation is for, and the author's
answer settles it: **random proximity connections and culling are the
initialization.** Sensing comes second, because the cull is what makes the
room to sense in. So the question is not whether the opening is indiscriminate
— it is meant to be — but whether the pond climbs back out.

It does. Seeded at 30,000, in windows, with `chance` at 0.22:

| window | frames | bodies | change | latches | commute share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 180 | 29,302 | −698 | 22,986 | 0.224 |

A clean U. It opens *at* chance — the dish is too crowded for steering to
decide anything — drops to 0.103 as the net works through the destructive
pairs that crowding made, then climbs back through the chance line to 0.251
while the bleed falls from 4,848 a window to 206. Two phases, exactly as the
design says: a proximity phase that culls, and a sensing phase in the space
the cull opened.

### The instrument: `Sim.rewriteMix`

Because that U is the shape worth watching, and nobody should have to drive a
console to see it, the measure is now a method. It reports the rewrite counts,
the commute share, **the share proximity alone would give for the current kind
census** (`detectRule` over the nine kind pairs, weighted by the census and
bucketed as `tally` buckets them), and the gap between them.

`selectivity` going positive is the moment latching stops being something that
happens to a body and starts being something it did. It is in the experiment
harness's `Sample` as `commuteShare`, `commuteChance` and `commuteEdge`, and
pinned by `rewrite-mix.test.ts`.

The baseline tracks the live census rather than the seed mix, which matters:
Eras erase, so a pond that has spent its Cons has a low chance line and a low
share for reasons that have nothing to do with how well it senses. Comparing
share against a fixed constant would read that as a collapse in selectivity.

### Where the frame stands

About 23 ms came off a 231 ms frame, or 10%. The ranking from §12 still holds
for what is left, with one correction: the pose pack is no longer the top
candidate, because two of the three packs have now had the cheap half of the
work taken out of them and what remains in them is the write itself.

The pattern across both passes is worth stating plainly for whoever picks this
up. **Look for work done more times than it needs to be, not for loops that
could be tighter.** Every win of consequence here — `disc_nei` eight times a
frame, `collect_pairs` eight times a frame, one CSR built four ways, one
endpoint resolution done twice — was the same shape. Every attempt to make a
per-body loop faster returned one to three milliseconds, and one of them
returned nothing at all.
